How the catalogue is built

Every artist on Plataforma has a catalogue: what they made, when, with whom, where it was shown, who holds it now. This document specifies that record — the structure, every field in it, and the reasoning behind each one.

Part one describes the model. Part two shows one record in full. Part three specifies every field that record can carry. The specimen throughout is Bronx Gothic, by sweat variant — the practice of Okwui Okpokwasili and Peter Born.

Part one — The model

A catalogue raisonné, while the artist is still here to correct it

The form being borrowed is the catalogue raisonné: the scholarly inventory of an artist's complete work. Conventionally it is a multi-volume book, compiled late in a career or after death, by someone other than the artist.

Plataforma builds it digitally, collaboratively, and while the artist is alive to verify and contextualise it. That changes what the structure has to do. A posthumous catalogue records settled facts. A living one holds an artist's own judgment about their work, including the judgments they revise — so every contested field is one the artist can set, and every automated finding is a proposal awaiting their confirmation.

One work, forty nights, two performers

For an object, the piece and the thing are the same. For performance they are not. Bronx Gothic is one work. It has played Los Angeles, Melbourne, Chicago, Rotterdam, Toulouse, Paris, Strasbourg, Zagreb, and a month at the Young Vic. It has been performed by Okwui Okpokwasili and, since 2022, by Wanjiru Kamuyu. A record with one level must call those one thing, which erases the differences, or forty things, which erases the work.

So the catalogue has five levels. Each exists because something true cannot be said without it.

A painting uses the same five levels and simply has fewer of them in play: one work, one realisation, one instance. Nothing about the structure itself is performance-specific.

The catalogue's unit is a practice, not a person

Bronx Gothic is not an Okpokwasili work with Born assisting. It is a Born/Okpokwasili work: written and performed by one, directed and designed by the other, with music by both. A structure that names one artist as primary and everyone else as a credit misattributes an entire category of contemporary practice — duos, collectives, companies, and long collaborations that outlast any single piece.

So the top-level entity is a practice, which may have one member or many. A solo artist is a practice of one; nothing about the record changes. Authorship within a work is a peer-level list — authors, each with their own roles — rather than one name with a supporting cast.

One record, with add-ons for each medium

A painting needs dimensions and a support. A performance needs a duration and a restaging policy. A film needs a runtime, an aspect ratio and a distributor. The obvious approach — a separate catalogue per medium — fails the artists Plataforma is for, almost none of whom work in one medium.

Instead there is a single work record carrying whatever modules apply: performance, object, installation, screen, text, design. A filmed installation of a live piece carries three, in one record, rather than being split across catalogues that never reconcile. The modules field declares which are present, so a reader knows which fields to expect.

Exhibitions and collections are entities, not fields

Grief and Grievance at the New Museum is a curatorial work containing many artists. Recorded as a line on one artist's work it is a footnote; recorded as its own entity it becomes a place where practices meet — the relationship that makes one artist's page a route to another's.

Ownership needs the same treatment. When the Hammer and the Whitney acquired poor people's tv room as an installation, what they acquired has terms: what may be displayed, loaned, reassembled, by whom. A free-text note cannot carry that. A collection record can, including the level at which the holding sits — a whole work, one realisation, or a single instance.

Identifiers

Scientists have ORCID: a free permanent identifier that follows a researcher across institutions, name changes and publications. Artists have nothing equivalent, so an artist's record fragments across every database holding a piece of it.

Plataforma reconciles rather than replaces. When an artist joins, the platform finds and links whatever already exists, and stores all of them — they answer different questions and no one of them is sufficient.

Most artists appear in none of them, and none of the ones they can join were built for artists. So every record also carries an identifier Plataforma mints itself, under three rules:

  • Permanent. Minted once, never reassigned, resolvable at a stable address even if the organisation, the domain or the software changes.
  • Meaningless. Not a name, not a year. Names get corrected and practices dissolve; an identifier that encodes a fact inherits that fact's expiry date.
  • Doubled by a readable handle. People need something recognisable (w-bg001). Records point at the permanent identifier underneath, so a correction to the handle never breaks a link.

Works can additionally carry a DOI — the citable permanent link used by scholarly publishing — which makes a catalogue entry quotable in a footnote the way a journal article is. That is a paid registration and a decision for later; the field is there for when it is made.

Stacked rights, and "we don't know yet" as a real answer

A production photograph involves at least two rights holders: the photographer, who owns the image, and the practice, who owns the work depicted. Reproducing it needs both. So documentation records carry stacked rights — a list of holders, each with the interest they hold — rather than a single owner field.

The same applies inside a work. The Audre Lorde passage in Bronx Gothic is not a note about the piece; it is material within it, and using it requires permission from her estate. It is recorded as incorporated material with its own rights status, separately from the artist's own statement about the work.

And clearance is allowed to be unresolved. For the Ian Douglas photographs the catalogue currently says unknown — needs confirmation. A field that can hold that is worth more than one that guesses: it makes the gap visible and actionable instead of inventing a permission nobody granted.

When is a work a new work?

Bronx Gothic: the oval was shown once, at River to River in June 2014. It is plainly derived from Bronx Gothic — and the artists' own archive gives it its own page, its own title, its own photographs. The catalogue can express either answer:

Option A — one work              Option B — two works, one derived

  Bronx Gothic  WORK               Bronx Gothic  WORK
        │                                │
   staged as                      transformed into
        │                                │
    the oval  REALISATION            the oval  WORK
                                           │
                                      staged as
                                           │
                                  River to River, 28 June 2014

The whole choice is one edge: whether the oval hangs under Bronx Gothic as a staging, or stands beside it as a work reached by a named derivation. Under option B it can hold its own credits, its own photographs, its own exhibition history. Everything else is identical.

Because derivation is a named relationship rather than a vague link, the catalogue can say how one work came from another: transformed-into, excerpted-from, expanded-into, installation-version-of, solo-version-of, study-for. Derivation is also a list, not a single link, because a piece can draw on several predecessors at once.

The harder case is poor people's tv room: a 2014 solo, a 2017 ensemble work, and a 2021 installation now held by the Hammer and the Whitney. The evidence points to three works in a chain — the credits diverge substantively, the 2014 solo predates what would otherwise be the canonical version, and institutional acquisition gives the installation a status the live work never had. That reading is an inference. Confirming it is the artists' call.

Three decisions that belong to Okwui and Peter

  1. **Is Bronx Gothic: the oval a separate work, or a staging of Bronx Gothic?** Their archive treats it as separate. Whatever they say is authoritative, and the record follows.
  2. **Is poor people's tv room one work or three?** Three, in a chain of derivation, is the current reading — inferred from credits and acquisitions, not confirmed.
  3. Is the 2021 installation autonomous now that museums own it? Acquisition changes a piece's standing. Whether it changes its identity is theirs to say.

See it working

The prototype is the artist's side of this: the platform searches, and the artist confirms, corrects, or rejects what it found. The structure never appears as structure — it shows up as an interface that knows what a restaging is.

  • What the platform found — the inversion the design rests on. Not "enter your first artwork" but "we found these, confirm?"
  • **the oval, live** — merge it into Bronx Gothic as a version, or keep it as a work of its own. The interface refuses to decide for her.
  • A record arriving wrong — poor people's tv room, with the wrong year and a missing co-author. One click to correct, and the correction is attributed.
  • A confident false match — Grief and Grievance is Okwui Enwezor's exhibition. Automated discovery will always produce these; the question is who gets the last word.

Documentation

Evidence of a work, a realisation or an instance — never the thing itself. The categories match how artists already organise their own archives, and they matter because different readers want different things: a scholar wants the essays, a presenter wants the technical documents, an audience wants the photographs.

What this catalogue is, and is not

It catalogues authored creative work, in any medium: performance, visual art, screen-based work, text, and design treated as authorship. It is aligned with the standards the surrounding world already uses — the library model of works and their realisations, Getty's vocabularies for materials and places, ORCID and Wikidata for people, DOI for citation — so the data travels.

It is not a universal cultural-heritage catalogue. It does not absorb commodities, mass-produced goods, natural objects or specimens, and it cross-references domain-specific catalogues rather than duplicating them. The boundary it enforces is authored creative intent.

Not yet attempted

  • How performance rights travel between people and institutions — who may restage a work, on what terms, once the originating practice is gone.
  • Conservation history for objects and installations.
  • What a museum owns when it acquires a re-assemblable installation. Genuinely unsolved in the field, not merely unsolved here.
  • Authentication methodology — the authenticationStatus vocabulary exists, but its legal terminology needs review before publication.
  • Participatory work, where the audience is arguably a co-author.
  • Abandoned works — begun, never finished, and still part of a practice.

Two principles run through every decision above, and they are the ones to argue with if any of it is wrong. First, the artist's judgment is authoritative: where a fact is contested, the structure holds their answer rather than resolving it automatically. Second, a record may be incomplete but never falsely confident — uncertain dates, unstacked rights and unconfirmed attributions are all sayable, because a catalogue that cannot admit a gap will invent one.
